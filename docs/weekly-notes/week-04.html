A join combines information from two tables using a shared key. For Census tables, the key is often GEOID.

Why GEOIDs should be text

A tract GEOID has 11 characters: 2 for the state + 3 for the county + 6 for the tract. The tract part is six characters, not four. A block-group GEOID adds one more character. Census GEOID guide

example_geoid <- "06001400100"
nchar(example_geoid)
[1] 11
as.numeric(example_geoid)
[1] 6001400100

The numeric conversion drops the leading zero. A GEOID identifies a place; it is not a quantity to calculate with. Store it as character text and use compatible key types on both sides of a join. Changing a damaged number back with as.character() does not restore a missing zero automatically.

R is case-sensitive: GEOID, Geoid, and geoid are different names. Function names are also case-sensitive: use left_join(), not Left_join().

Spatial Data: A Table Plus Geometry

An sf object is a data frame with a special geometry column and spatial metadata. It can hold points, lines, polygons, or their multi-feature versions. The geometry records the coordinates; the other columns hold attributes such as income or population.

  • A bike-share station can be a POINT.
  • A street can be a LINESTRING.
  • A tract can be a POLYGON or MULTIPOLYGON.

A polygon needs a closed boundary made from several coordinates. A single coordinate pair is a point, not a complete polygon.

sf stands for simple features. It is an R package and an object representation. Shapefiles, GeoJSON, and GeoPackages are storage formats that sf can read or write; they are not the same thing as an sf object. Introduction to sf

Three related names appear in R: sf is the complete spatial table, sfc is its geometry list-column, and sfg represents one geometry. st_geometry(x) extracts the geometry, while st_drop_geometry(x) gives us the attributes without geometry. These are different operations.

Income Reliability and Our First Map

We retain tracts with a positive population estimate, as in the lab. We also keep the original unfiltered object for checking joins later.

philly_tracts <- tracts_2023_all %>%
  filter(total_popE > 0) %>%
  mutate(
    med_inc_se = if_else(
      !is.na(med_incM) & med_incM >= 0,
      med_incM / 1.645,
      NA_real_

These are the lab’s CV categories: below 12%, 12% through 40%, and above 40%. They are not the Week 2 categories based on moe_pct. CV and relative MOE differ by the factor 1.645. The labels summarize relative sampling uncertainty; they do not turn an estimate into a certainty.

Why a successful join is not enough for change over time

Tracts can split, merge, or change boundaries between Census geography vintages. The 2015 and 2023 ACS products straddle the 2020 tract revisions. A missing match is a reason to investigate; even a matching GEOID does not prove that the geographic footprint stayed identical.

left_join() cannot resolve those boundary changes. A spatial join alone cannot resolve them either. Comparing the same places over time can require geographic relationship files or a documented harmonization method. We also need to express income in comparable inflation-adjusted dollars and account for uncertainty. Median incomes cannot simply be added or averaged to reconstruct a new tract’s median. Coordinate Reference Systems

A coordinate reference system, or CRS, explains what coordinates mean: their reference framework, axes, units, and, where applicable, projection. Numbers alone do not tell us whether a point is in Philadelphia, whether its units are feet, or whether its coordinates are longitude and latitude.

The geoid, ellipsoid, and datum

The recording includes the background behind these terms:

  • The geoid is a gravity-based reference surface approximating mean sea level, extended under the continents. It is not a model of every mountain and valley.
  • An ellipsoid is a smooth mathematical Earth model, slightly flattened at the poles. It gives us a manageable surface for coordinates.
  • A geodetic datum or reference frame connects that model and its coordinates to locations on Earth.
  • A geographic CRS adds a coordinate system, axes, and angular units to that reference framework. A datum alone is not the entire CRS.

The lowercase geoid in this discussion is completely different from the Census GEOID identifier. One is a reference surface; the other is a code in our data table. NOAA’s geoid explanation

The historical example from class was NAD27: it uses the Clarke 1866 ellipsoid and a reference origin at Meades Ranch, Kansas. NAD83 uses GRS80 and was developed with a geocentric reference framework. The same numeric longitude and latitude can therefore refer to different ground positions under different datums. WGS84 is another widely used global reference system associated with GPS. NOAA’s datum FAQ

The practical lesson is to inspect the full CRS, not just check whether both layers use degrees. A datum mismatch can create a location offset even when two layers look almost aligned. The size of that offset depends on the systems and location; there is no universal correction to add by hand.

Geographic and projected coordinates

Family Typical coordinates Units Main point
Geographic Longitude and latitude Degrees Angular positions on an Earth model
Projected Easting and northing Often meters or feet Planar coordinates designed for a region or purpose

A degree of longitude does not have the same ground length at every latitude. A buffer distance of 0.5 is therefore not automatically half a mile. For this lab, a local projected CRS makes distances and areas easier to interpret.

Geographic coordinates can still support geodesic distance calculations with appropriate software. The point is to understand the calculation and units, not to assume every operation in longitude and latitude is invalid.

In the common signed decimal-degree convention, longitude runs from -180 to 180 and latitude from -90 to 90. A Philadelphia coordinate near longitude -75 and latitude 40 is plausible. Those ranges are useful clues, not proof of a CRS. Confirm the metadata. In typical sf point construction, x is longitude and y is latitude when supplying longitude/latitude coordinates.

Projections preserve different properties

Common projection families include cylindrical, transverse cylindrical, conic, and azimuthal (planar). These describe how a projection is constructed. A separate question is what it preserves.

The class demonstration used an orange and a sheet of paper to explain flattening a curved surface. It is a useful analogy for distortion, although many projections are mathematical transformations rather than literal shadows from a light. Tangent and secant constructions help explain reference lines or points where scale is controlled. A projection works best in its intended region, not equally well everywhere.

Property Meaning Caution
Conformal Preserves local angles and shapes at very small scales Does not preserve area; Lambert Conformal Conic is an example
Equal-area Preserves area relationships Useful when area comparison matters; shapes can be distorted
Equidistant Preserves specified distances, such as from a chosen center Does not preserve every distance everywhere
Direction Some projections preserve bearings from a chosen center No flat map preserves every direction, area, and distance globally

Mercator is conformal and shows constant-bearing routes, or rhumb lines, as straight lines. That does not mean every straight line is the shortest route, or that all distances and directions between arbitrary points are preserved.

EPSG 2272 is specifically the US survey-foot version. It is not “feet or meters” interchangeably. A meter-based State Plane definition uses a different CRS code.

UTM divides the world into 60 numbered longitudinal zones, with northern and southern hemisphere versions. Zone numbers run from 1 to 60; there are no negative zone numbers. The hemisphere and datum matter as well as the zone. Large easting and northing values are normal and do not mean the coordinates are wrong.

Inspect, transform, or label?

Function What it does
st_crs(x) Reads the object’s CRS metadata
st_transform(x, 2272) Converts coordinates from their known source CRS to the target CRS
st_set_crs(x, 4326) Assigns or replaces a CRS label without moving the coordinates

The normal workflow is check the CRS, then transform if needed. There is no extra st_set_crs() step after st_transform().

The recording’s jar analogy helps: putting a “salt” label on a jar of sugar does not change what is inside. Likewise, calling st_set_crs(x, 2272) on longitude/latitude numbers does not convert them into feet. It only gives those same numbers a different interpretation. st_transform() performs the coordinate conversion and updates the label together.

Only use st_set_crs() when reliable information establishes what the existing coordinates already mean. Assigning a label to unknown coordinates by guesswork can place the data in the wrong location. sf transformation reference

Spatial Operations: Match by Location

Table joins ask whether keys match. Spatial operations ask whether geometries have a relationship, such as intersecting, touching, containing, or being within a specified distance.

Predicates answer a relationship question

Function Relationship tested
st_intersects(x, y) The features share any point, including a boundary point
st_touches(x, y) The boundaries meet, but the interiors do not intersect
st_within(x, y) Each feature in x lies within a feature in y, according to the predicate’s boundary rules
st_contains(x, y) The corresponding containment question with the roles reversed
st_disjoint(x, y) The features share no points
st_is_within_distance(x, y, dist = ...) Their minimum distance is within the supplied distance

st_touches() is more specific than everyday “touches.” A tract overlapping the inside of a station buffer intersects it; it usually does not satisfy st_touches(). Use st_intersects() when “any overlap or contact” is the intended rule. sf spatial predicates

For the classroom polygon example, imagine polygon B beside A with a shared edge, C entirely inside A, and D separated from A:

Relationship to A Intersects A? Touches A? Within A?
B shares an edge, with no interior overlap TRUE TRUE FALSE
C is entirely inside A, away from its boundary TRUE FALSE TRUE
D is separated from A FALSE FALSE FALSE

“Intersects” is the broad test. It includes containment as well as boundary contact and partial overlap. “Touches” excludes interior overlap.

The default result of many predicates is a sparse list of matching row positions. For example, the first element of st_intersects(tracts, stations) tells us which station rows intersect the first tract. lengths() counts matches for every element of that list. The singular length() would count the list’s elements, usually the number of input tracts.

Filter, join, or change the shapes?

Operation Result Does it cut the original shapes?
st_filter(x, y) Selected whole features from x No
st_join(x, y) Features of x with matched attributes from y No; rows may repeat for multiple matches
st_intersection(x, y) Shared geometric portions Yes
st_difference(x, y) Portions of x outside y Yes
st_sym_difference(x, y) Portions belonging to one side but not both Yes
st_union(x) Combined geometry with internal overlap dissolved Merges shapes
st_distance(x, y) Distances between features Returns measurements, not a new polygon layer

st_union() combines geometric coverage; it does not automatically add population or compute an income statistic. Likewise, st_intersection() can copy attributes to fragments, but it does not automatically estimate how many residents live in each fragment. sf geometry operations

Names can differ in meaning across GIS software. In this lab, the one-input st_union(x) dissolves the geometries together. Do not assume it produces the same attribute-preserving polygon subdivision as a desktop GIS tool named “Union.”

The steps are:

  1. Measure each original tract’s area.
  2. Cut the tract at the station-zone boundary.
  3. Measure the retained portion and sum any fragments for that GEOID.
  4. Drop geometry from the coverage table.
  5. Join the measurements back to the original whole tracts.
  6. Divide covered area by original area and multiply by 100.

Both areas use the same squared units, so the units cancel in the percentage. A tract with no intersecting portion has zero covered area. We join only one coverage record per GEOID to prevent duplicated tract rows.

We select only GEOID before the intersection because we need a spatial measurement, not copied income estimates on each fragment. An income estimate does not become an estimate for the clipped fragment just because its polygon was cut.

What We Can and Cannot Conclude

  • Income: a tract median describes households collectively. It does not establish that every resident is low-income.
  • Sampling uncertainty: CV and MOE help describe uncertainty in the ACS estimate. They do not account for every possible source of error.
  • Location: an intersecting station, a nearby centroid, and a large covered area measure different things.
  • Travel: straight-line proximity does not measure walking time, safe crossings, or cycling conditions.
  • Service: a station point does not guarantee available bikes, available docks, affordable membership, or reliable service. The feed’s totalDocks field, if present, could support a separate capacity question.
  • Time: the station snapshot is contemporary to its download; the ACS estimates describe 2019–2023. This is not a reconstruction of 2023 service unless we obtain historical station data.
  • Boundaries: joining records is not the same as making different geography vintages comparable.

These checks help distinguish a useful screening map from a stronger claim about who Indego actually serves.

My notes from September 18, 2026, reduce the workflow to a few checks I want to remember:

  • Join by the same GEOID. A left_join() keeps the left table’s rows and attaches matching columns. If a key does not match, the new value is NA; check the key type, duplicates, and row count.
  • Remember that geometry sticks. Selecting columns from an sf object usually keeps its geometry. Use st_drop_geometry() when I only need an ordinary attribute table.
  • Read the CRS before measuring. Geographic coordinates use longitude and latitude in degrees. Projected coordinates use eastings and northings in a local unit such as feet or meters. For Philadelphia, EPSG 2272 is NAD83 / Pennsylvania South in US survey feet; 5,280 feet make a mile.

  • Choose the spatial operation by the question. Predicates such as st_intersects() test a relationship; st_filter() keeps whole features; st_intersection() cuts to shared areas; st_union() dissolves overlaps; st_join() attaches attributes by location.
  • Check the shape of distance output. st_distance(x, y) normally returns distances for every pair as a matrix. Find the nearest feature first and use by_element = TRUE when I need one matched distance per tract.

## Joining Tables: Match by an Identifier

A join combines information from two tables using a shared **key**. For Census tables, the key is often `GEOID`.

### The example from class

These are small invented tables to make the joins easy to follow. The three-digit identifiers are teaching examples, not real tract GEOIDs.

```{r}
#| label: example-tables
income_23_example <- tibble(
  GEOID = c("100", "200", "300"),
  med_inc_23 = c(82000, 31500, 45000)
)

income_15_example <- tibble(
  GEOID = c("100", "200", "400"),
  med_inc_15 = c(61000, 27000, 39000)

income_23_example
income_15_example
```

```{r}
#| label: example-left-join
left_join(income_23_example, income_15_example, by = "GEOID")
```

The result is:

| GEOID | med_inc_23 | med_inc_15 |
|:------|-----------:|-----------:|
| 100 | 82000 | 61000 |
| 200 | 31500 | 27000 |
| 300 | 45000 | NA |

The third identifier stays **300** because it came from the left table. It does not become 400. There is no 2015 match for 300, so its new income column contains `NA`. The 2015-only row for 400 is excluded.

Read `left_join(x, y, by = "GEOID")` as: **keep the rows from `x`, find matching keys in `y`, and bring over their columns.**

### Reading the Venn diagram

The photograph from class illustrates **table joins**. Think of the circles as sets of identifiers. The shaded left circle includes identifiers with and without a match; the shared middle contains identifiers found in both tables; both circles together contain all identifiers.

| Join | Rows kept | Identifiers in our example |
|:-----|:----------|:---------------------------|
| `left_join(x, y)` | All left-table rows, with any matching right-table data | 100, 200, 300 |
| `inner_join(x, y)` | Rows with a match in both tables | 100, 200 |
| `full_join(x, y)` | Rows from both tables, including unmatched rows | 100, 200, 300, 400 |
| `right_join(x, y)` | All right-table rows, with any matching left-table data | 100, 200, 400 |
| `anti_join(x, y)` | Left-table rows with no match in the right table | 300 |
| `semi_join(x, y)` | Left-table rows that have a match; no new columns | 100, 200 |

```{r}
#| label: compare-table-joins
inner_join(income_23_example, income_15_example, by = "GEOID")
full_join(income_23_example, income_15_example, by = "GEOID")
anti_join(income_23_example, income_15_example, by = "GEOID")
```

The circles are a memory aid, but real joins match **rows**, not just sets. If a key appears twice in the right table, one matching left row can become two output rows. A left join retains every left row, but it does not always retain the original row count. Swapping the inputs of a left join keeps the other table's rows, which is why we often use a left join instead of switching to a right join. [dplyr join reference](https://dplyr.tidyverse.org/reference/mutate-joins.html)

### Why GEOIDs should be text

A tract GEOID has 11 characters: **2 for the state + 3 for the county + 6 for the tract**. The tract part is six characters, not four. A block-group GEOID adds one more character. [Census GEOID guide](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/geography/guidance/geo-identifiers.html)

```{r}
#| label: geoid-text
example_geoid <- "06001400100"
nchar(example_geoid)
as.numeric(example_geoid)
```

The numeric conversion drops the leading zero. A GEOID identifies a place; it is not a quantity to calculate with. Store it as character text and use compatible key types on both sides of a join. Changing a damaged number back with `as.character()` does not restore a missing zero automatically.

R is case-sensitive: `GEOID`, `Geoid`, and `geoid` are different names. Function names are also case-sensitive: use `left_join()`, not `Left_join()`.

## Spatial Data: A Table Plus Geometry

An `sf` object is a data frame with a special geometry column and spatial metadata. It can hold points, lines, polygons, or their multi-feature versions. The geometry records the coordinates; the other columns hold attributes such as income or population.

- A bike-share station can be a **POINT**.
- A street can be a **LINESTRING**.
- A tract can be a **POLYGON** or **MULTIPOLYGON**.

A polygon needs a closed boundary made from several coordinates. A single coordinate pair is a point, not a complete polygon.

`sf` stands for **simple features**. It is an R package and an object representation. Shapefiles, GeoJSON, and GeoPackages are storage formats that `sf` can read or write; they are not the same thing as an `sf` object. [Introduction to sf](https://r-spatial.github.io/sf/articles/sf1.html)

Three related names appear in R: `sf` is the complete spatial table, `sfc` is its geometry list-column, and `sfg` represents one geometry. `st_geometry(x)` extracts the geometry, while `st_drop_geometry(x)` gives us the attributes without geometry. These are different operations.

```{r}
      NA_real_
```

The ACS MOE is normally reported at the 90% confidence level, so we use the conversion in the [Census accuracy worked examples](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs/tech_docs/accuracy/2024_ACS_Accuracy_Document_Worked_Examples.pdf):

\[
SE = MOE / 1.645, \qquad CV = (SE / estimate)\times100.
\]

These are the lab's **CV** categories: below 12%, 12% through 40%, and above 40%. They are not the Week 2 categories based on `moe_pct`. CV and relative MOE differ by the factor 1.645. The labels summarize relative sampling uncertainty; they do not turn an estimate into a certainty.

### Why a successful join is not enough for change over time

Tracts can split, merge, or change boundaries between Census geography vintages. The 2015 and 2023 ACS products straddle the 2020 tract revisions. A missing match is a reason to investigate; even a matching GEOID does not prove that the geographic footprint stayed identical.

`left_join()` cannot resolve those boundary changes. A spatial join alone cannot resolve them either. Comparing the same places over time can require geographic relationship files or a documented harmonization method. We also need to express income in comparable inflation-adjusted dollars and account for uncertainty. Median incomes cannot simply be added or averaged to reconstruct a new tract's median. [Census geography relationship files](https://www.census.gov/geographies/reference-files/2020/geo/relationship-files.html)

## Coordinate Reference Systems

A **coordinate reference system**, or CRS, explains what coordinates mean: their reference framework, axes, units, and, where applicable, projection. Numbers alone do not tell us whether a point is in Philadelphia, whether its units are feet, or whether its coordinates are longitude and latitude.

### The geoid, ellipsoid, and datum

The recording includes the background behind these terms:

- The **geoid** is a gravity-based reference surface approximating mean sea level, extended under the continents. It is not a model of every mountain and valley.
- An **ellipsoid** is a smooth mathematical Earth model, slightly flattened at the poles. It gives us a manageable surface for coordinates.
- A **geodetic datum or reference frame** connects that model and its coordinates to locations on Earth.
- A **geographic CRS** adds a coordinate system, axes, and angular units to that reference framework. A datum alone is not the entire CRS.

The lowercase **geoid** in this discussion is completely different from the Census **GEOID** identifier. One is a reference surface; the other is a code in our data table. [NOAA's geoid explanation](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/geoid.html)

The historical example from class was NAD27: it uses the Clarke 1866 ellipsoid and a reference origin at Meades Ranch, Kansas. NAD83 uses GRS80 and was developed with a geocentric reference framework. The same numeric longitude and latitude can therefore refer to different ground positions under different datums. WGS84 is another widely used global reference system associated with GPS. [NOAA's datum FAQ](https://geodesy.noaa.gov/datums/faq.shtml)

The practical lesson is to inspect the full CRS, not just check whether both layers use degrees. A datum mismatch can create a location offset even when two layers look almost aligned. The size of that offset depends on the systems and location; there is no universal correction to add by hand.

### Geographic and projected coordinates

| Family | Typical coordinates | Units | Main point |
|:-------|:--------------------|:------|:-----------|
| Geographic | Longitude and latitude | Degrees | Angular positions on an Earth model |
| Projected | Easting and northing | Often meters or feet | Planar coordinates designed for a region or purpose |

A degree of longitude does not have the same ground length at every latitude. A buffer distance of `0.5` is therefore not automatically half a mile. For this lab, a local projected CRS makes distances and areas easier to interpret.

Geographic coordinates can still support geodesic distance calculations with appropriate software. The point is to understand the calculation and units, not to assume every operation in longitude and latitude is invalid.

In the common signed decimal-degree convention, longitude runs from -180 to 180 and latitude from -90 to 90. A Philadelphia coordinate near longitude -75 and latitude 40 is plausible. Those ranges are useful clues, not proof of a CRS. Confirm the metadata. In typical `sf` point construction, `x` is longitude and `y` is latitude when supplying longitude/latitude coordinates.

### Projections preserve different properties

Common projection families include cylindrical, transverse cylindrical, conic, and azimuthal (planar). These describe how a projection is constructed. A separate question is **what it preserves**.

The class demonstration used an orange and a sheet of paper to explain flattening a curved surface. It is a useful analogy for distortion, although many projections are mathematical transformations rather than literal shadows from a light. Tangent and secant constructions help explain reference lines or points where scale is controlled. A projection works best in its intended region, not equally well everywhere.

| Property | Meaning | Caution |
|:---------|:--------|:--------|
| Conformal | Preserves local angles and shapes at very small scales | Does not preserve area; Lambert Conformal Conic is an example |
| Equal-area | Preserves area relationships | Useful when area comparison matters; shapes can be distorted |
| Equidistant | Preserves specified distances, such as from a chosen center | Does not preserve every distance everywhere |
| Direction | Some projections preserve bearings from a chosen center | No flat map preserves every direction, area, and distance globally |

Mercator is conformal and shows constant-bearing routes, or rhumb lines, as straight lines. That does not mean every straight line is the shortest route, or that all distances and directions between arbitrary points are preserved.

### CRS codes used in class

| EPSG | CRS | Units | Useful context |
|-----:|:----|:------|:---------------|
| 2272 | NAD83 / Pennsylvania South (ftUS) | **US survey feet** | Local analysis around Philadelphia |
| 5070 | NAD83 / Conus Albers | Meters | Equal-area work across the contiguous United States |
| 4326 | WGS 84 | Degrees | Common geographic coordinates |
| 4269 | NAD83 | Degrees | Common in US geographic datasets |
| 26918 | NAD83 / UTM zone 18N | Meters | A projected option for Philadelphia |

EPSG 2272 is specifically the US survey-foot version. It is not "feet or meters" interchangeably. A meter-based State Plane definition uses a different CRS code.

UTM divides the world into 60 numbered longitudinal zones, with northern and southern hemisphere versions. Zone numbers run from 1 to 60; there are no negative zone numbers. The hemisphere and datum matter as well as the zone. Large easting and northing values are normal and do not mean the coordinates are wrong.

### Inspect, transform, or label?

| Function | What it does |
|:---------|:-------------|
| `st_crs(x)` | Reads the object's CRS metadata |
| `st_transform(x, 2272)` | Converts coordinates from their known source CRS to the target CRS |
| `st_set_crs(x, 4326)` | Assigns or replaces a CRS label without moving the coordinates |

The normal workflow is **check the CRS, then transform if needed**. There is no extra `st_set_crs()` step after `st_transform()`.

The recording's jar analogy helps: putting a "salt" label on a jar of sugar does not change what is inside. Likewise, calling `st_set_crs(x, 2272)` on longitude/latitude numbers does not convert them into feet. It only gives those same numbers a different interpretation. `st_transform()` performs the coordinate conversion and updates the label together.

Only use `st_set_crs()` when reliable information establishes what the existing coordinates already mean. Assigning a label to unknown coordinates by guesswork can place the data in the wrong location. [sf transformation reference](https://r-spatial.github.io/sf/reference/st_transform.html)

## Spatial Operations: Match by Location

Table joins ask whether keys match. Spatial operations ask whether geometries have a relationship, such as intersecting, touching, containing, or being within a specified distance.

### Predicates answer a relationship question

| Function | Relationship tested |
|:---------|:--------------------|
| `st_intersects(x, y)` | The features share any point, including a boundary point |
| `st_touches(x, y)` | The boundaries meet, but the interiors do not intersect |
| `st_within(x, y)` | Each feature in `x` lies within a feature in `y`, according to the predicate's boundary rules |
| `st_contains(x, y)` | The corresponding containment question with the roles reversed |
| `st_disjoint(x, y)` | The features share no points |
| `st_is_within_distance(x, y, dist = ...)` | Their minimum distance is within the supplied distance |

`st_touches()` is more specific than everyday "touches." A tract overlapping the inside of a station buffer **intersects** it; it usually does not satisfy `st_touches()`. Use `st_intersects()` when "any overlap or contact" is the intended rule. [sf spatial predicates](https://r-spatial.github.io/sf/reference/geos_binary_pred.html)

For the classroom polygon example, imagine polygon B beside A with a shared edge, C entirely inside A, and D separated from A:

| Relationship to A | Intersects A? | Touches A? | Within A? |
|:------------------|:-------------|:-----------|:----------|
| B shares an edge, with no interior overlap | TRUE | TRUE | FALSE |
| C is entirely inside A, away from its boundary | TRUE | FALSE | TRUE |
| D is separated from A | FALSE | FALSE | FALSE |

"Intersects" is the broad test. It includes containment as well as boundary contact and partial overlap. "Touches" excludes interior overlap.

The default result of many predicates is a sparse list of matching row positions. For example, the first element of `st_intersects(tracts, stations)` tells us which station rows intersect the first tract. `lengths()` counts matches for every element of that list. The singular `length()` would count the list's elements, usually the number of input tracts.

### Filter, join, or change the shapes?

| Operation | Result | Does it cut the original shapes? |
|:----------|:-------|:--------------------------------|
| `st_filter(x, y)` | Selected whole features from `x` | No |
| `st_join(x, y)` | Features of `x` with matched attributes from `y` | No; rows may repeat for multiple matches |
| `st_intersection(x, y)` | Shared geometric portions | Yes |
| `st_difference(x, y)` | Portions of `x` outside `y` | Yes |
| `st_sym_difference(x, y)` | Portions belonging to one side but not both | Yes |
| `st_union(x)` | Combined geometry with internal overlap dissolved | Merges shapes |
| `st_distance(x, y)` | Distances between features | Returns measurements, not a new polygon layer |

`st_union()` combines geometric coverage; it does not automatically add population or compute an income statistic. Likewise, `st_intersection()` can copy attributes to fragments, but it does not automatically estimate how many residents live in each fragment. [sf geometry operations](https://r-spatial.github.io/sf/reference/geos_binary_ops.html)

Names can differ in meaning across GIS software. In this lab, the one-input `st_union(x)` dissolves the geometries together. Do not assume it produces the same attribute-preserving polygon subdivision as a desktop GIS tool named "Union."

## Distance to the Nearest Station

### A tract needs a representative point

```{r}
#| label: tract-centroids
centroids <- st_centroid(st_geometry(philly_tracts))
```

We use each tract's geometric centroid as a simple starting location. A centroid is not necessarily where most residents live, and it can fall outside an irregular polygon. `st_point_on_surface()` gives a point inside the polygon, but that point is not automatically a population center either.

### All pairs versus one pair per tract

```{r}
#| label: distance-matrix
distance_matrix <- st_distance(centroids, stations)
dim(distance_matrix)
distance_matrix[
  seq_len(min(3, nrow(distance_matrix))),
  seq_len(min(3, ncol(distance_matrix))),
  drop = FALSE
]
```

The rows represent tract centroids and the columns represent stations. Each cell is the distance for that pair. These measurements carry units. Without `by_element = TRUE`, this is a full matrix, not one distance per tract. Large all-pairs matrices can be expensive, so the next approach is more useful when we only need the nearest station. [sf distance measurements](https://r-spatial.github.io/sf/reference/geos_measures.html)

The geometries I pass in change the question. `st_distance(philly_tracts, stations)` measures the minimum distance between each **polygon** and each station, so a station inside a tract has distance zero from that tract. Using `centroids` instead measures from its representative point. Distance to a tract's boundary or footprint and distance from its centroid are not interchangeable.

```{r}
#| label: nearest-station
nearest_idx <- st_nearest_feature(centroids, stations)

philly_tracts <- philly_tracts %>%
  mutate(
    nearest_station = stations$name[nearest_idx],
    dist_ft = as.numeric(
      st_distance(
        centroids,
        stations[nearest_idx, ],
        by_element = TRUE
      )
    ),
    dist_mi = dist_ft / 5280
  )

summary(philly_tracts$dist_mi)
```

`nearest_idx` contains station **row positions**, not distances. `stations[nearest_idx, ]` selects one matching station per tract, in tract order. `by_element = TRUE` then compares the first centroid with the first selected station, the second with the second, and so on.

If I were sorting distances manually, the nearest would be the **smallest** distance: ascending order. `arrange(desc(distance))` would put the farthest first. `st_nearest_feature()` avoids building and sorting a full distance table for this task.

`as.numeric()` removes the units label after we have checked that the CRS uses feet. Dividing by 5,280 expresses the distance in the lab's miles. These are straight-line distances, not walking routes. Rivers, rail yards, highways, entrances, and bridges can make a short straight-line distance a much longer journey.

## Half-Mile Buffers: Filter Whole Tracts or Cut Their Shapes

A buffer creates an area within a specified distance of a feature. Here each station gets a 2,640-foot circle. Unioning the buffers combines overlapping circles so shared areas are not counted repeatedly.

```{r}
  st_buffer(dist = 2640) %>%
  st_union()

ggplot() +

  geom_sf(data = station_zone, fill = "#56B4E9", color = NA, alpha = 0.5) +

    title = "Half-mile zones around Indego stations",
    subtitle = "Overlapping station buffers have been dissolved into one geometry"
  )
```

The union can contain separated pieces in a MULTIPOLYGON. "One geometry" does not mean every station circle connects to every other one. [sf buffers and other unary operations](https://r-spatial.github.io/sf/reference/geos_unary.html)

### st_filter(): keep the whole tract

```{r}
#| label: filter-buffer
tracts_near_stations <- st_filter(philly_tracts, station_zone)

outside_zone <- st_filter(
  philly_tracts,
  station_zone,
  .predicate = st_disjoint
)

tibble(
  relationship = c("Any intersection with zone", "No intersection with zone"),
  n_tracts = c(nrow(tracts_near_stations), nrow(outside_zone))

```

The default filter predicate is `st_intersects()`. A tract with even a tiny overlap, or just a shared boundary point, is selected whole. Its geometry is not trimmed to the buffer.

If the question instead asks for tracts entirely within the zone, make that predicate explicit:

```{r}

entirely_within_zone <- st_filter(
  philly_tracts,
  station_zone,
  .predicate = st_within
)
nrow(entirely_within_zone)
```

`st_filter()` returns selected features. `nrow()` counts them. The function does not return a count by itself, and it does not require full containment unless we choose that relationship.

We use the **unioned** station zone for the disjoint test. Filtering for disjointness against individual station buffers could retain a tract simply because it is far from one station, even if it is beside another.

### st_intersection(): keep only the covered portion

```{r}
#| label: tract-coverage
philly_tracts <- philly_tracts %>%
  mutate(tract_area = as.numeric(st_area(.)))

covered <- philly_tracts %>%
  select(GEOID) %>%
  st_intersection(station_zone) %>%
  mutate(covered_area = as.numeric(st_area(.))) %>%
  st_drop_geometry() %>%
  group_by(GEOID) %>%
  summarize(covered_area = sum(covered_area), .groups = "drop")

philly_tracts <- philly_tracts %>%
  left_join(covered, by = "GEOID") %>%
  mutate(
    covered_area = replace_na(covered_area, 0),
    pct_covered = if_else(
      tract_area > 0,
      100 * covered_area / tract_area,
      NA_real_
    )
  )

stopifnot(!anyDuplicated(philly_tracts$GEOID))
summary(philly_tracts$pct_covered)
```

The steps are:

1. Measure each original tract's area.
2. Cut the tract at the station-zone boundary.
3. Measure the retained portion and sum any fragments for that GEOID.
4. Drop geometry from the coverage table.
5. Join the measurements back to the original whole tracts.
6. Divide covered area by original area and multiply by 100.

Both areas use the same squared units, so the units cancel in the percentage. A tract with no intersecting portion has zero covered area. We join only one coverage record per GEOID to prevent duplicated tract rows.

We select only `GEOID` before the intersection because we need a spatial measurement, not copied income estimates on each fragment. An income estimate does not become an estimate for the clipped fragment just because its polygon was cut.

Some `sf` versions print a warning that attributes are assumed to be spatially constant when performing an overlay. Here the retained attribute is just the tract identifier. We are measuring clipped geometry, not assuming that income or population is evenly distributed inside the tract.

```{r}

  geom_sf(aes(fill = pct_covered), color = NA) +
  scale_fill_viridis_c(
    labels = function(x) paste0(round(x), "%"),
    na.value = "grey80"
  ) +
  theme_void() +
  labs(
    title = "Tract area within half a mile of an Indego station",
    subtitle = "Area coverage is not the percentage of residents served",
    fill = "Area covered"
  )
```

A tract could be half covered by area while most homes are in its uncovered half. Water, industrial land, parks, and uneven settlement matter. This is an area-based indicator, not a direct resident-access rate.

## Worked Challenge: Which Low-Income Tracts Lack Access?

For this example:

- **Low income:** a tract's 2019–2023 median household income is at or below the 25th percentile among populated Philadelphia tracts with a known positive estimate.
- **Access:** the tract centroid is within half a mile of its nearest station.

This is a relative citywide income definition, not an official poverty threshold. Ties at the cutoff can put slightly more than 25% of eligible tracts in the group. Missing income stays unknown and is not treated as high income.

```{r}
#| label: challenge-definitions
known_income <- philly_tracts$med_incE[
  !is.na(philly_tracts$med_incE) & philly_tracts$med_incE > 0
]
stopifnot(length(known_income) > 0)

income_cutoff <- unname(quantile(known_income, probs = 0.25))

philly_tracts <- philly_tracts %>%
  mutate(
    low_income = case_when(
      is.na(med_incE) | med_incE <= 0 ~ NA,
      TRUE ~ med_incE <= income_cutoff
    ),
    access_centroid = dist_mi <= 0.5,
    access_any_overlap = lengths(st_intersects(., station_zone)) > 0,
    access_half_area = pct_covered >= 50
  )

scales::dollar(income_cutoff)

philly_tracts %>%
  st_drop_geometry() %>%
  summarize(
    total_tracts = n(),
    income_status_unknown = sum(is.na(low_income)),
    low_income_tracts = sum(low_income, na.rm = TRUE),
    low_income_without_centroid_access = sum(
      low_income & !access_centroid,
      na.rm = TRUE
    )
  )
```

The cutoff is calculated from this dataset. It will print above rather than being guessed in the notes. `na.rm = TRUE` excludes unknown values from the count; the separate unknown count makes that exclusion visible.

## What We Can and Cannot Conclude

- **Income:** a tract median describes households collectively. It does not establish that every resident is low-income.
- **Sampling uncertainty:** CV and MOE help describe uncertainty in the ACS estimate. They do not account for every possible source of error.
- **Location:** an intersecting station, a nearby centroid, and a large covered area measure different things.
- **Travel:** straight-line proximity does not measure walking time, safe crossings, or cycling conditions.
- **Service:** a station point does not guarantee available bikes, available docks, affordable membership, or reliable service. The feed's `totalDocks` field, if present, could support a separate capacity question.
- **Time:** the station snapshot is contemporary to its download; the ACS estimates describe 2019–2023. This is not a reconstruction of 2023 service unless we obtain historical station data.
- **Boundaries:** joining records is not the same as making different geography vintages comparable.

These checks help distinguish a useful screening map from a stronger claim about who Indego actually serves.

### My September 18 class-note recap

My notes from September 18, 2026, reduce the workflow to a few checks I want to remember:

- **Join by the same GEOID.** A `left_join()` keeps the left table's rows and attaches matching columns. If a key does not match, the new value is `NA`; check the key type, duplicates, and row count.
- **Remember that geometry sticks.** Selecting columns from an `sf` object usually keeps its geometry. Use `st_drop_geometry()` when I only need an ordinary attribute table.
- **Read the CRS before measuring.** Geographic coordinates use longitude and latitude in degrees. Projected coordinates use eastings and northings in a local unit such as feet or meters. For Philadelphia, EPSG 2272 is NAD83 / Pennsylvania South in US survey feet; 5,280 feet make a mile.
- **Do not confuse labeling with conversion.** `st_crs()` checks the metadata, `st_transform()` converts coordinates, and `st_set_crs()` only assigns a label to coordinates whose CRS is already known.
- **Choose the spatial operation by the question.** Predicates such as `st_intersects()` test a relationship; `st_filter()` keeps whole features; `st_intersection()` cuts to shared areas; `st_union()` dissolves overlaps; `st_join()` attaches attributes by location.
- **Check the shape of distance output.** `st_distance(x, y)` normally returns distances for every pair as a matrix. Find the nearest feature first and use `by_element = TRUE` when I need one matched distance per tract.

Those reminders connect my class shorthand to the full examples above. For example, a half-mile buffer in EPSG 2272 uses 2,640 feet, and the chosen predicate determines whether a tract merely touches the zone, overlaps it, or lies within it.
