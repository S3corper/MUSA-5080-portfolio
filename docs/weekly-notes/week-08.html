Week 8: Local Errors, Spatial Features, and RealPage

Simple notes on finding clustered errors and using predictions responsibly.
Author

Leo Leung

Published

October 23, 2026

These standalone notes summarize Dr. Elizabeth Delmelle’s October 23, 2026 lecture. Numerical results below come from the supplied lecture text.

1. What is this week about?

A model can perform well overall but repeatedly make mistakes in the same neighborhood.

  • Find the mistakes: use global Moran’s I and LISA.
  • Investigate them: measure a missing neighborhood feature and test it.
  • Consider the consequences: ask who is affected by the predictions.

2. Start with prediction errors

\[ \text{Residual} = \text{actual price} - \text{predicted price} \]

For a house that sells for $300,000:

Prediction Residual Meaning
$250,000 +$50,000 Underprediction: the prediction is too low
$350,000 −$50,000 Overprediction: the prediction is too high

Use out-of-fold residuals: predict each house using a model trained without that house’s validation group. This checks how well the model predicts data it did not train on.

3. Global Moran’s I and LISA

Spatial autocorrelation means nearby observations have related values. Here, we are studying nearby prediction errors.

Method What it gives Question it answers
Global Moran’s I One number for the dataset Do similar errors cluster overall?
Local Moran’s I (used in LISA) One number per house Where are the clusters and unusual houses?

LISA means Local Indicators of Spatial Association. The lecture uses each house’s five nearest sales as its neighbors.

The basic calculation

Local Moran’s I multiplies a house’s centered residual by its neighbors’ average centered residual, then divides by a common scale. A centered residual is the residual minus the mean residual.

  • Same signs: a positive local value, suggesting similar errors nearby.
  • Opposite signs: a negative local value, suggesting a house differs from its neighbors.

The lecture’s five-house example gives an average local value of 0.72, equal to global Moran’s I under the same row-standardized weights. These weights average the neighbors. Different weights can give a different result, so keep weights consistent when comparing models.

Read the four groups

The first word describes the house; the second describes its neighbors.

Group Meaning What to investigate
High–High High residual among high residuals A missing neighborhood amenity
Low–Low Low residual among low residuals A missing neighborhood disadvantage
High–Low High residual among low residuals An unusual house or incorrect data
Low–High Low residual among high residuals An unusual house or incorrect data

High and low are relative to the mean residual. If that mean is near zero, High–High roughly means a pocket of underprediction, and Low–Low roughly means a pocket of overprediction.

High–High and Low–Low are clusters. High–Low and Low–High are spatial outliers. Neither proves the cause of an error.

The lecture uses 499 random permutations and a 0.01 significance threshold to flag patterns. With thousands of houses, some flags can occur by chance. “Not significant” does not mean a prediction is accurate.

4. Turn “near” into a measurable feature

Operationalizing means turning an idea into a number. For example, “near violent incidents” can be measured in three ways:

Method Example Limitation
Buffer count Number of incidents within 660 feet Depends on the chosen radius
Nearest distance Distance to the closest incident One incident can dominate
kNN mean distance Average distance to the five closest incidents Depends on the chosen number of neighbors

More incidents produce a higher count. Closer incidents produce a smaller distance. Use coordinates with suitable distance units; the lecture uses feet.

Logging a distance emphasizes proportional changes: 100 to 500 feet is a much bigger proportional change than 5,000 to 5,400 feet. Check that distances are positive before taking their logarithms.

Choose a few reasonable radii or neighbor counts, then compare cross-validation RMSE using the same rows and folds. RMSE measures prediction error; lower is better.

5. Did adding vacancy help?

The lecture tests whether neighborhood vacancy explains Low–Low pockets.

Model CV RMSE Global Moran’s I Low–Low houses
House features + incident kNN distance $432,287 0.260 536
Same model + tract vacancy $431,883 0.258 705

Prediction error and overall clustering decrease slightly, but more houses are classified as Low–Low. Adding vacancy did not reduce the number of flagged Low–Low houses. A better overall score does not guarantee better local results.

Use this workflow:

  1. Fit a model and calculate out-of-fold residuals.
  2. Check global Moran’s I and map the LISA groups.
  3. Ask what might explain a cluster.
  4. Measure that idea and add it as a feature.
  5. Refit and compare prediction error and local patterns.

6. Why do these errors matter?

If predictions were used for property assessments, overprediction could mean an excessive tax burden.

In the lecture, 4.48% of sales in the lowest tract-income group were Low–Low, compared with 0.459% in the highest group. This suggests uneven error patterns worth investigating. These are model results, not actual tax bills; tract income also does not tell us an individual household’s income.

7. RealPage: should we use the model?

The lecture describes RealPage software that recommends rents using public information, landlords’ own records, and competitors’ private rental data.

The debate concerns sharing competitors’ private information and how freely managers can reject recommendations. Critics argue that this can help landlords coordinate prices. Defenders emphasize efficiency and managers’ control over final prices.

The classroom bidding exercise asks whether a consulting firm should accept, modify, or reject requests such as pooling private rent rolls, accepting recommendations by default, and maximizing revenue. It asks students to distinguish what they can build from what they are willing to sell.

Three questions guide the discussion:

  1. Where does the data come from? Public information and competitors’ private records raise different concerns.
  2. Who makes the decision? Can a manager meaningfully reject the recommendation?
  3. Who bears the consequences? Renters may be harmed even when the model accurately predicts what they will pay.

Accuracy alone does not make a model’s use fair or beneficial.
